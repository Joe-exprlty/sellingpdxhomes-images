Portland, Oregon — A certified home inspector examines a crawlspace during a standard pre-purchase inspection. Most Portland homes over 40 years old warrant at least one specialty add-on beyond the general inspection.

You found the home. You made the offer. The seller accepted. Now comes the part most buyers underestimate — the inspection period. In Portland, a standard general inspection is just the starting point. The city's housing stock, climate, and soil geology create inspection issues you will not read about in a national home-buying guide. This post covers what to expect from a Portland inspection, how to read the report, which specialty inspections actually matter here, and what to negotiate — and when to walk away.

Quick Answer

What should Portland home buyers know about the inspection process?

A general home inspection in Portland costs $400-$600 and covers the structure, systems, and visible conditions of the home. Oregon law gives buyers a specific inspection contingency period — typically 10 business days — to review findings and negotiate or terminate. Portland's older housing stock and wet climate make four specialty inspections especially important: sewer scope, oil tank locate, radon test, and mold/moisture assessment. These are add-ons your general inspector does not perform, and each addresses a condition that is more common here than in most U.S. cities. After the inspection, buyers can negotiate credits, repairs, or price reductions on material defects — or terminate the contract and recover their earnest money.

→ Bottom line: A Portland home inspection is not a pass/fail test — it is a negotiating tool. The buyers who use it well walk into closing with either a fair price or a seller credit that accounts for real conditions in the home.

What a General Home Inspection Covers

A general home inspection in Oregon must be performed by an Oregon Certified Home Inspector (OCHI), licensed through the Oregon Construction Contractors Board. Your inspector will walk through the property and evaluate the condition of the roof, foundation, exterior, interior, HVAC systems, plumbing, electrical, windows and doors, attic, and any accessible crawlspace or basement. The report that follows is typically 30-80 pages with photos and detailed findings.

General inspections cost $400-$600 in the Portland metro for a standard single-family home. Larger homes, homes with detached structures, or homes with unusual systems may run higher. Most inspections take 2.5 to 4 hours on-site. You should be there for the full inspection — walking through with your inspector at the end is one of the highest-value hours you will spend in the home-buying process.

The general inspection covers visible and accessible conditions. It does not include inside walls, underground systems, or specialty environmental hazards. That is where the add-ons come in. Oregon's CCB licensing requirements for home inspectors set the floor for what a certified inspection must cover — your inspector should be happy to walk you through what is and is not within the scope of a general inspection before you book.

The Inspection Timeline in Oregon

Once your offer is accepted, the Oregon Real Estate and Inspection (OREI) addendum activates your inspection contingency. The default inspection period in Oregon is 10 business days from acceptance, though this is negotiable. In a competitive Portland market, offers sometimes come in with 7-day inspection periods to appeal to sellers. In slower periods, 14 days is common and reasonable.

Portland-Specific Inspections You Should Add

Portland's housing stock, geology, and climate create four inspection categories that come up regularly in local transactions and rarely appear in national home-buying guides. Each one is an add-on ordered separately from your general inspection. None of them are required by law. All four are worth at least asking about on every Portland purchase — which ones you actually order depends on the age and type of the home.

Select the inspection type relevant to your home:

Data Point

Sewer Scope Inspection

Cost: $175-$275 | Who performs it: Sewer scope specialist (separate from general inspector) | Best for: Any Portland home built before 1985

Source: Portland Bureau of Environmental Services, local contractor ranges

A sewer scope sends a camera down the lateral sewer line — the pipe that runs from your home to the city main — to look for root intrusion, bellied sections, cracks, or collapsed pipe. Portland has a significant inventory of pre-1960 homes with clay lateral lines, and the root systems of Portland's mature street trees are aggressive. Root intrusion is the most common finding on older Portland laterals.

The Portland Bureau of Environmental Services (BES) is responsible for the city main. You are responsible for the lateral from your foundation to that main — typically 30 to 100 feet of pipe depending on the lot. A simple root intrusion catch at inspection costs $3,000-$6,000 to spot-repair. A fully collapsed or bellied lateral that needs replacement runs $10,000-$20,000+, and those repairs are yours the moment you close.

The sewer scope is one of the most consistently underordered inspections in Portland buyer transactions. It is not included in the general inspection, it costs around $200, and it surfaces problems that have no visible above-ground signal. On any pre-1985 home, order it as a default — not as an if-the-inspector-flags-something optional.

What a bad finding looks like: The scope video shows roots growing through pipe joints (roots will look like wisps of vegetation coming through the walls of the pipe), a belly (the pipe dips and holds water), or active cracking and separation. Your specialist will give you a condition rating and a repair recommendation with the report.

How to negotiate it: A root intrusion finding is a real cost the seller either knew about or should have maintained. A credit at closing or a price reduction equivalent to the repair estimate is the standard ask. Sellers do not typically refuse if the scope footage is clear.

Data Point

Oil Tank Locate (Tank Sweep)

Cost: $125-$200 for the locate | Who performs it: DEQ-licensed UST service provider | Best for: Any Portland home built between 1920 and 1975

Source: Oregon DEQ UST Service Provider Program (rules updated July 1, 2025)

Portland has a large inventory of homes that heated with fuel oil before converting to gas or electric heat. When those conversions happened — often in the 1960s and 70s — the underground storage tanks (USTs) were frequently left in place rather than decommissioned. An oil tank locate uses a magnetometer to detect buried metal under the yard, driveway, or basement floor.

Oregon DEQ updated its UST contractor licensing rules effective July 1, 2025. All work performed on regulated UST systems in Oregon must now be completed by a licensed DEQ service provider. The DEQ's "Your DEQ Online" portal (launched January 2025) handles all notifications, reporting, and permitting for tank work. When you order an oil tank locate, confirm your specialist holds a current DEQ license.

If a tank is found: Step one is soil sampling around the tank perimeter to test for contamination. A clean tank that was properly decommissioned at the time of conversion may have minimal contamination. An older abandoned tank with degraded walls can have contamination in the soil. Cleanup costs range from $5,000-$10,000 for a clean decommission to $50,000+ if contamination has migrated into the soil profile or toward drainage.

Oregon disclosure law: Sellers are required to disclose known USTs on the property disclosure form. However, the seller may genuinely not know one exists — particularly if they purchased the home after the heating conversion. That is not a disclosure violation. It is just why you order the locate regardless of what the seller's disclosure says.

How to negotiate it: When a tank is found, sellers typically agree to decommission before closing or provide a credit covering the estimated decommission cost. DEQ documentation of a properly decommissioned tank is the clean outcome. Sellers rarely walk away from an oil tank negotiation if the issue is properly documented with a licensed contractor's estimate, because leaving an undisclosed tank in the sale creates liability for them too.

Data Point

Radon Test

Cost: $150-$250 professional test | Who performs it: Certified radon tester | Best for: All Portland homes, especially Northeast Portland and homes with basements or slab foundations

Source: Oregon Health Authority Radon Awareness Program; EPA radon action level guidance

Portland has an above-average radon problem. The Oregon Health Authority reports that 28% of Portland homes test above the EPA action level of 4.0 pCi/L — compared to roughly 6% nationally. The source is Portland's geology: the Missoula Floods deposited uranium-containing sediment throughout the Willamette Valley, and that sediment releases radon gas that seeps into homes through foundations, walls, and crawlspace gaps.

The OHA classifies Portland's 33 ZIP codes by radon risk level. 11 are classified as high risk. 18 are classified as moderate risk. Northeast Portland has the highest concentration of high-risk ZIPs. The OHA publishes an Oregon radon risk map by ZIP code — look up the specific address before closing. The OHA also offers free short-term test kits for residents in under-tested ZIP codes.

Radon testing is not required under Oregon law as part of a home sale. It is entirely optional and buyer-initiated. The EPA recommends short-term tests (2-7 days) for real estate transactions. The test device is placed in the lowest livable level of the home — usually the basement or a ground-floor room.

If radon tests above 4.0 pCi/L: A sub-slab or crawlspace depressurization system (the standard mitigation approach) costs $1,500-$3,500 installed in Portland. This is a predictable, fixed cost — and sellers almost always credit it or agree to install it before closing because the number is verifiable and the fix is reliable. A properly installed mitigation system typically reduces radon levels by 90%+.

Key note for your negotiation: The OHA recommends all Oregon homes test for radon regardless of ZIP code risk level because radon levels can vary dramatically from block to block and house to house. Do not skip the test because the ZIP code is green.

Data Point

Mold & Moisture Assessment

Cost: $300-$600 assessment; $100-$300 per sample for lab air testing | Who performs it: Mold/moisture specialist | Best for: Homes with crawlspaces, older vapor barriers, or general inspector flags on moisture

Source: EPA mold remediation guidance; local Portland contractor ranges

Portland's climate — wet winters from October through May — creates real mold risk in crawlspaces, basements, and anywhere water management has failed over time. The PNW mold pattern is different from humid-heat mold found in the Southeast: Portland mold tends to be moisture-driven and structural rather than air-quality driven. Crawlspace joist mold is the most common finding in Portland older-home transactions.

A standard general inspection covers visible mold and moisture damage. Hidden mold — inside wall cavities, under flooring, in the HVAC system — is not within the scope of a general inspection. If your inspector notes any moisture intrusion, visible mold, or high moisture readings with a moisture meter, order a dedicated mold assessment before the contingency window closes.

Common findings and costs:

  • Cosmetic surface mold (bathroom tile grout, surface of a basement wall): $300-$1,500 to remediate
  • Crawlspace mold on joists (common in Portland): $3,000-$12,000 depending on extent; vapor barrier replacement is typically included
  • Structural mold in wall cavities (rare, requires opening walls): $5,000-$30,000+ depending on how far it has spread

Vapor barriers: Portland homes with updated crawlspace vapor barriers have dramatically fewer mold surprises. A well-encapsulated crawlspace is a selling point. An old or torn vapor barrier in a home with a wet crawlspace is the most reliable predictor of mold on the joists above it.

How to negotiate it: Mold remediation costs are a legitimate inspection item. Document the finding with photos, get a remediation estimate from a certified contractor, and submit the estimate as part of your Buyer's Inspection Response. Sellers typically credit mold remediation when the finding is clearly documented, because refusing a documented mold issue creates disclosure risk for them in a future sale. EPA's mold remediation guidance gives buyers useful context on what documented remediation actually involves.

How to Read the Report and What to Negotiate

Oregon inspection reports use a condition rating system to categorize findings: safety hazard, deficient, requires monitoring, or informational. Not every flagged item is a negotiating point. A 25-year-old water heater that is functioning normally is informational — it has age and will eventually need replacement, but it is not a deficiency today. Active water intrusion from a compromised roof is a deficiency. Knowing the difference is where your agent's experience matters.

The dollar threshold that shapes Portland negotiations: In Portland transactions, buyers typically focus their repair requests on items estimated above $1,500-$2,000. Below that threshold, most experienced agents recommend absorbing the cost rather than risking a seller walking over a minor item. It is not that small things do not matter — it is that every negotiation carries relationship risk, and using leverage on a $300 item can poison a conversation about a $6,000 item.

What Portland sellers typically offer vs. refuse:

  • Roof with less than 5 years of useful life remaining: Sellers often credit. Roof replacement is a known, estimable cost and sellers know a future buyer will face the same issue.
  • Deferred cosmetic maintenance (peeling paint, caulk failure, minor hardware): Sellers almost always refuse to credit. These are maintenance items the purchase price accounts for, and sellers correctly view them as not their problem.
  • Active water intrusion (identified source — roof penetration, failed flashing, foundation crack with moisture path): Negotiable. Depends on whether the source can be identified and the repair estimated clearly.
  • Oil tank decommission (documented by a licensed DEQ contractor): Sellers typically credit or decommission pre-closing. Refusing a documented oil tank issue creates liability for them in any future sale.
  • Radon mitigation (tested above 4.0 pCi/L): Sellers frequently credit the mitigation system cost because it is predictable ($1,500-$3,500) and the remediation is reliable. This is one of the smoother inspection negotiations in Portland transactions.
  • Sewer lateral repairs (root intrusion, belly documented by scope footage): Negotiable. With scope footage as documentation, sellers understand the evidence is visible and the repair is real.

In my Portland transactions, the inspection requests that go sideways are almost always the ones that list too many items. A seller who receives a 14-item repair list feels attacked and gets defensive. A seller who receives a 2-item list with documented costs and a clean rationale usually negotiates. Pick your two biggest items, make the case clearly, and you will get further.

It can request: specific repairs completed by a licensed contractor before closing, a seller credit applied at closing (reducing the amount you bring to the table), a price reduction, or a combination. Credit vs. Repair: Which to Ask For

When your inspection turns up a legitimate negotiating item, you have two main paths: ask the seller to fix it before closing, or ask for a credit at closing that you use to fix it yourself.

Prefer Credits When
  • You have a contractor you trust for the work
  • The repair involves cosmetic choices (paint color, fixture style)
  • You want control over the quality of workmanship
  • The seller's timeline is tight and repair quality could suffer
  • The repair is predictable in cost (radon mitigation, vapor barrier)
Prefer Repairs When
  • The issue is a safety hazard that must be fixed regardless
  • The scope of work could expand and a credit might not cover it
  • Lender requirements mandate the repair before closing (FHA, VA)
  • Decommissioning an oil tank with DEQ documentation attached

The general rule: credits give you more control. Seller repairs are done to sell the home, not to live in it. A credit means you pick the contractor, you define the scope, and you verify the quality. There is a limit — lenders sometimes flag large seller credits if they push above allowable concession percentages — but for most Portland transactions, a credit is cleaner than requiring a repair.

One nuance worth knowing: credits reduce the seller's net proceeds but do not change the purchase price on paper. Price reductions change the purchase price, which affects your loan amount and any calculations tied to it. If you are close to a loan limit threshold or working with down payment assistance, confirm with your lender before deciding between a credit and a price reduction. For a detailed look at how seller credits flow through to your final numbers, the Oregon closing costs guide covers exactly how these transactions settle at the table.

When to Walk Away

Not every inspection finding is a negotiating item. Some findings are a reason to terminate. Knowing the difference before you get into a difficult inspection conversation is what separates buyers who close confidently from buyers who overpay for a home with a real problem, or who walk away from a home that was actually fine.

Scenarios where termination is the right call:

  • Active structural compromise: Failing foundation that has moved or cracked in a way that indicates active ongoing movement — not historical settling, but current and continuing. If a structural engineer (ordered after your general inspection) concludes the foundation requires major remediation, the cost and complexity frequently exceed what a seller will credit.
  • Active sewage backup discovered at inspection: Not a partial root intrusion — a sewer line that cannot function. The repair cost is significant and the timeline is uncertain.
  • Significant undisclosed oil contamination with migration: A decommissioned tank with clean soil is a routine finding. A leaking tank with contamination that has migrated toward a drainage channel or neighboring property is a DEQ cleanup situation that can take years and cost six figures. If the scope of contamination is not determinable within your inspection window, that is a walk scenario.
  • Systemic electrical hazards that cannot be estimated: Knob-and-tube wiring throughout the home, ungrounded panels, or aluminum wiring in a post-1970 home that has been poorly modified. These are insurable but expensive and sometimes difficult to scope during inspection.

Terminating within the inspection period means your earnest money comes back in full, per the OREI contingency. The process moves quickly: your agent submits the OREI termination notice before the deadline, the escrow company processes the earnest money release, and you start looking at the next home. There is no penalty. That protection exists specifically for this purpose.

Exception

When Standard Inspection Strategy Does Not Apply

New construction: Builder contracts often limit or waive the standard Oregon inspection contingency. Many builders allow a third-party inspection but not a negotiation period in the traditional sense. Review the builder's contract carefully with your agent before signing — the OREI form does not govern new construction builder contracts.

As-is sales: Some Portland listings are sold as-is, particularly estate sales and bank-owned properties. An as-is sale does not waive your right to inspect — you can still perform a full inspection and the specialty add-ons. What it means is the seller has stated upfront they will not negotiate on findings. You still retain the right to terminate within the inspection period. The inspection gives you the information to decide whether the as-is price is fair given the actual condition.

Condos: Condo inspections are scoped differently. You typically inspect only the interior of the unit — the shell, systems serving only your unit, and any exclusive-use areas. The common elements (roof, exterior, shared systems) are the HOA's responsibility. Order a review of the HOA's reserve study and financial statements alongside the inspection, not as part of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to get a home inspection in Oregon?

Oregon law does not require a home inspection as part of a residential purchase transaction. However, almost every purchase contract written by a buyer's agent includes an inspection contingency using the OREI form, which gives you the right to inspect and negotiate. Waiving the inspection contingency is possible — buyers sometimes do it in highly competitive multiple-offer situations — but it means accepting the home's condition as-is with no right to negotiate or terminate based on findings. In most Portland transactions, maintaining the inspection contingency is the right call.

How much does a home inspection cost in Portland, Oregon?

A standard general home inspection in Portland runs $400-$600 for most single-family homes. Larger homes or homes with detached structures may run higher. Specialty inspections are add-ons: sewer scope $175-$275, oil tank locate $125-$200, radon test $150-$250, mold/moisture assessment $300-$600. If you order all four specialty inspections — which is reasonable on an older Portland home — budget an additional $750-$1,325 beyond the general inspection. That total is still a small fraction of what any one of those findings could cost you post-closing without the information.

How long does a home inspection take in Oregon?

A general inspection takes 2.5 to 4 hours for a standard single-family home. Larger homes, homes with basements and attics, or older homes with more complex systems take longer. Specialty inspections are typically scheduled on a different day — a sewer scope takes 30-60 minutes, an oil tank locate takes 1-2 hours, a radon test requires the device to sit for 48-96 hours before results. Plan for all inspections to be completed within the first 6-7 days of your 10-day contingency window so you have time to review and submit your Buyer's Inspection Response before the deadline.

Should I be at the home inspection in Portland?

Yes. Walking through with your inspector at the end of the on-site inspection is one of the highest-value uses of your time in the entire buying process. Inspectors explain findings in person in ways that a written report cannot — they can show you exactly where the moisture reading was high, what the water heater looks like, and which items they consider significant versus minor. You will read the report more confidently and ask better questions of your agent if you were there. It also builds a relationship with the inspector if you need follow-up questions answered during the negotiation.

What is a sewer scope and do I need one in Portland?

A sewer scope is a camera inspection of the lateral sewer line — the pipe running from your home to the city main. It checks for root intrusion, bellied pipe (a section that dips and holds water), cracks, or collapse. In Portland, with a large inventory of pre-1960 homes and aggressive root systems from mature street trees, a sewer scope is one of the most consistently useful add-ons. It costs $175-$275 and surfaces problems that have no visible above-ground signal. A lateral replacement discovered post-closing costs $10,000-$20,000+. The scope is worth it on any Portland home built before 1985.

How common is radon in Portland homes?

More common than most buyers expect. The Oregon Health Authority reports that 28% of Portland homes test above the EPA action level of 4.0 pCi/L — roughly four times the national average. The geology of the Willamette Valley creates above-average radon risk throughout the Portland metro, with Northeast Portland having the highest concentration of high-risk ZIP codes. Radon is invisible and odorless, and testing is not required by Oregon law, which means many homeowners simply do not know. A short-term professional radon test costs $150-$250. If the result is above 4.0 pCi/L, a mitigation system runs $1,500-$3,500 and reduces radon levels by 90%+.

Can I negotiate after a home inspection in Oregon?

Yes. Oregon's inspection contingency process gives buyers the right to submit a Buyer's Inspection Response requesting credits, repairs, or a price reduction based on findings. You can also terminate the contract and recover your earnest money in full if you do so before the contingency deadline. Negotiation typically focuses on material defects with documented costs — not cosmetic maintenance items. The strongest negotiating positions are ones where the finding is documented with a licensed contractor's report or estimate and the requested credit is directly tied to that estimate. Your agent's role during this period is to help you prioritize, draft the response, and negotiate the outcome.

## 1. STORY SENTENCE

September 2026 is the month rates took the wheel: the 30-year fixed climbed from 6.71% to 7.03% across the month and hit 7.28% on October 1 after the Fed's first rate hike since 2023, and Portland buyers responded by signing the fewest September contracts since 2010 (pending sales down 17.9%) and making the fewest September lockbox opens on record, yet the median price held exactly at last year's $545,000, which tells us sellers have not blinked and the adjustment so far is happening in volume and time, not in price.

## 5. THREE TO SIX MONTH TREND (April through September 2026)

- **Inventory broke out, then kept going.** 3.1, 3.2, 3.1, 3.3, 3.8, 4.2. Two straight months of 0.4 to 0.5-month jumps after five months inside a 0.3-month band.
- **Active listings resumed growth.** After easing in August, actives rose to 7,411, the 2026 high. Total active listings including proposed and under construction reached 8,974, also the 2026 high and above September 2025's 8,341 (RMLS page 7).
- **Pending sales down 27.8% from the May peak** (2,439 to 1,760). Five consecutive months of decline. The September step-down (-12.6%) was more than double last year's August-to-September drop (-5.7%, 2,275 to 2,145).
- **New listings held steady** (2,440 to 2,487) while pendings fell. Sellers did not pull back further in September; buyers did.
- **Median price stalled in a $540K to $565K band** all year from April forward: $550,000, $560,000, $564,900, $555,000, $540,000, $545,000. Down 3.5% from the June peak, up 6.9% from January's $510,000.
- **Market time turned up sharply.** 54 days in July, 57 in August, 68 in September. The +11 days month over month is far steeper than last year's flat August-to-September (62 to 62).
- **List-to-sale gap widened back to last year's level.** Sale prices ran about 1.3% below list in September (RMLS page 6 chart read), versus about 0.5% below in August. Last month's edge for sellers over 2025 has closed.
- **Price per square foot steady around $311** (chart read), essentially even with September 2025.
- **Showings decelerated every month since February** and the decline is accelerating. See Section 3.
- **Mortgage rates rose every week of September**: 6.71%, 6.76%, 6.95%, 7.03%, then 7.28% on October 1. That is a 57-basis-point move in five weeks.

## 7. AFFORDABILITY SNAPSHOT

Median priced home, September 2026: **$545,000**
20% down payment: $109,000
Loan amount: $436,000
Rate: 6.86%, the average of Freddie Mac's four September 2026 weekly readings (6.71%, 6.76%, 6.95%, 7.03%)

**Monthly principal and interest: $2,861**

Taxes, homeowners insurance, and maintenance are additional and not included.

**Year-over-year comparison:** the identical $545,000 median at September 2025's average of 6.35% (Freddie Mac weekly readings 6.50%, 6.35%, 6.26%, 6.30%) produced $2,714 per month. Buyers are now paying **$147 more per month**, about $1,760 a year, for the same median-priced home. Price did not change. The rate did all of it.

**At today's rate:** 7.28% on the same $436,000 loan is **$2,983 per month**, $122 above the September average and **$270 a month above a year ago**.

**This is the reversal.** In July buyers were $76 a month ahead of the prior year. In August they were $19 ahead. In September they are $147 behind, and at October's opening rate, $270 behind. Three months took the affordability advantage from positive to clearly negative.

**Income needed:** at a 28% front-end ratio counting principal and interest only, about $122,600 a year at 6.86%, and about $127,900 at 7.28%. HUD puts 2026 median family income for the metro at $128,300. At today's rate, a median-income household is right at the edge on P&I alone, before taxes and insurance.

**Trend:** RMLS affordability index at 95%, up roughly 2 points from the same quarter last year. That reading uses a 6.7% rate, so it overstates current conditions.

## 8. EXTERNAL CONTEXT (8 categories)

**1. Mortgage rate environment.** Freddie Mac PMMS 30-year fixed was **7.28% on October 1, 2026**, up 25 basis points from 7.03% the prior week and up from 6.34% a year earlier, the highest reading since October 2023 (per FinancialJuice/Real Deal coverage of the release). The 15-year fixed was 6.60%. September 2026 weekly readings: 6.71% (9/3), 6.76% (9/10), 6.95% (9/17), 7.03% (9/24), monthly average 6.86%. Sam Khater, Freddie Mac chief economist, October 1: "With mortgage rates on their current trajectory, the housing market continues to be supported by favorable economic conditions." **Key change: rates crossed 7% for the first time in this report series and are now nearly a full point above a year ago.**

**2. Federal Reserve policy.** On September 16, 2026 the FOMC **raised** the federal funds target range by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4.00%, the first increase since July 2023. The vote was 12-0. The statement: "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." Chair Kevin Warsh, who took office in May 2026: "The plain fact is that inflation is too high, and has been for too long" (via Chase/CNBC coverage). August CPI rose 0.4% for the month and 3.4% year over year, with crude oil above $100 a barrel. The median dot plot shows 4.1% at end of 2026, signaling one more hike this year. Next meetings: October 27-28 and December 8-9, 2026. The July dissenters who wanted a hike got their way two months later.

**3. Regional employment.** Oregon's unemployment rate edged down to 5.1% in August 2026 from 5.2% in July, versus 4.1% nationally (Oregon Employment Department, released September 16, 2026). Payrolls fell 800 jobs in August and are down 10,900 (-0.5%) over the year. Construction added 700 and retail 500; professional and business services lost 1,100. Over 12 months, health care added 10,200 jobs while manufacturing lost 6,800 (-3.8%). Employment economist Gail Krumenauer to OPB on manufacturing: "wood products had several publicly announced closures," though losses "have flattened in recent months." I did not find a new Intel or Nike workforce announcement dated within the reporting window that I could verify, so neither appears as a fresh data point.

**4. Oregon housing legislation.** No new state housing legislation passed during September; the 2026 short session ended in March. The bills from the Oregon Real Estate Agency's 2026 legislative update remain the relevant ones: **HB 4128** (90-day head start for families and small buyers before private equity can purchase single-family homes), **SB 1551** (voids deed restrictions blocking fire-hardened building materials), **HB 4082** (urban growth boundary expansions for manufactured and senior housing), and **SB 1521** (Portland-MSA cities must offset developer costs before mandating affordable units, phasing in 2028). Note: last month's package said "a batch of new Oregon laws takes effect September 26, 2026." The KGW article behind that appears to be the September 26, **2025** effective date (a Friday; September 26, 2026 is a Saturday). I recommend not repeating that claim. See Flag D3.

**5. Portland metro economic context.** The structural picture is unchanged from last month: the Portland Metro Chamber's 2026 State of the Economy found the region lost 8,800 jobs in 2025 (fourth worst among U.S. metros), and Kidder Mathews reports Q2 2026 office vacancy at 15.3% with positive year-to-date absorption and downtown CBD vacancy at 27.7%. The new statewide data (Category 3) shows the job base still shrinking slowly rather than falling sharply. No Q3 office report was available as of this package.

**6. National market context.** NAR's August 2026 existing-home sales (released September 10): 3.98 million annual rate, -2.0% month over month and -1.2% year over year; national median $429,100, +1.6%; **4.9 months of supply**, which Lawrence Yun called "its highest level in over ten years"; 31 days on market. The West: 720,000 annual rate (flat MoM, -2.7% YoY), median $619,100 (-0.2%). S&P Cotality Case-Shiller for July 2026 (released September 29): national +1.9%, 20-city +2.5%, 10-city +3.4%, **Portland -0.65%**, with Chicago strongest at +6.9% and Seattle weakest at -1.6%. Rebecca Kaufman, S&P DJI: "While home prices continued to decline in real terms in July 2026, marking the 14th consecutive month of real declines, slightly lower inflation and stronger nominal home price appreciation helped narrow the gap." NAHB/Wells Fargo HMI fell 3 points to 32 in September; 38% of builders cut prices and 66% used incentives.
*Portland read: Portland's Case-Shiller dipped back to -0.65% in July after narrowing to -0.38% in June, still underperforming the nation. At 4.2 months, Portland's inventory is now below the national 4.9 months of supply for August, so Portland is not uniquely oversupplied; the softness is a national rate story landing on a market that was already flat.*

**7. Consumer platform directional check.** Realtor.com's September 2026 housing report (released September 30): 20.8% of U.S. listings had a price cut, the highest September since 2018. **Portland ranked third among the 50 largest metros at 31.6%**, behind Salt Lake City (33.6%) and Denver (32.1%); the West was 22.8%. Nationally, active inventory rose 5.4% year over year, pending listings fell 4.1%, and the median list price fell 1.4%, the 11th straight annual decline. Portland's share rose from 30.5% in August. This aligns with RMLS: rising inventory, falling pendings, longer market time, and a list-to-sale gap that widened. **Source Convergence callout is warranted**, cited as Tier 3 corroboration only.

**8. Tariffs and construction costs.** NAHB's September HMI release cites "rising material costs" alongside higher rates as builders' main headwinds; NAHB Chairman Bill Owens: "Buyer traffic has weakened across much of the country, largely because of rising mortgage rates." NAHB's standing tariff estimate remains roughly $10,900 per new home, with Canadian softwood lumber at a combined burden near 45%. No new tariff action affecting residential materials was announced in September that I could verify. For Portland, higher material costs plus 7% financing keep new construction slow, which limits how fast new-build supply can add to the resale inventory build.
